## 2. Problem Statement

### 2.1 Background

Obesity is a chronic disease that arises from interacting environmental, psychosocial and biological factors. According to the World Health Organization, 2.6 billion adults were living with overweight in 2024, of whom 940 million were living with obesity, and worldwide adult obesity has more than doubled since 1990 [1]. Because the drivers of excess weight differ between individuals, the European Association for the Study of Obesity (EASO) recommends person-centred, flexible medical nutrition therapy rather than a single prescribed diet [2].

Mobile health (mHealth) applications are a scalable way to deliver such personalized support in daily life. Their measured benefit, however, is modest and depends strongly on what they are compared with. A meta-analysis of 19 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of stand-alone digital lifestyle interventions found a small overall effect on weight-related outcomes (standardized mean difference [SMD] 0.26), no significant anthropometric effect for diet-only programs, and control type as the only significant moderator: wait-list controls produced the largest effects, while active controls produced no significant effect [3].

### 2.2 Problem

Three limitations of current digital weight-management tools motivate this project:

1. **Unproven added value of automated personalization.** In the 12-month SMARTER trial (n = 502), tailored feedback on self-monitoring did not improve weight loss over self-monitoring alone (−2.12% vs −2.39%, P = .68), although it improved calorie-goal adherence [4]. Large effects are mostly reported against wait-list controls [3], [5].
2. **Personalization of one behavior at a time.** Nutrition-only personalized interventions have been short and produced small weight changes [6], [7]; activity-focused adaptive prompts increased steps in the hour after a prompt but not weekly activity [8]. Few systems jointly personalize nutrition and physical activity.
3. **Opaque or unverified recommendations and poorly defined engagement.** Menus generated by a general-purpose AI chatbot for a simulated person with obesity contained less energy than requested, unbalanced macronutrients and insufficient calcium [10]. Among 59 nutrition-app studies, only 31% defined engagement explicitly and 29% measured retention [9].

As a result, a user who wants to manage their weight without clinical supervision has no transparent tool that (a) identifies which of their own modifiable lifestyle factors contribute most to excess weight, (b) turns these factors into joint, guideline-constrained nutrition and activity recommendations, and (c) explains every recommendation. Whether such a system improves engagement and weight-related outcomes over plain self-monitoring has not been tested in a controlled study, and none of the ten original studies reviewed in the Midterm Research Proposal was conducted in Central Asia.

### 2.3 Definition of "obesity factors" in this project

The project addresses **modifiable lifestyle factors** that can be measured by a smartphone application and acted upon without medical supervision:

| Factor group | Indicators collected by the system |
|---|---|
| Dietary intake | Energy intake relative to the personal target; macronutrient distribution; diet-quality components; calcium intake |
| Physical activity | Daily steps relative to the personal baseline; activity intensity (when a wearable is connected) |
| Sedentary behavior | Prolonged sitting periods |
| Anthropometric status (monitored, not treated) | Body weight, BMI, waist circumference |

Genetic, clinical and socioeconomic factors are acknowledged as drivers of obesity [1], [2] but are not assessed by the system (see Section 7.2). "Identifying obesity factors" therefore means detecting, for each user, which modifiable factors deviate most from guideline-based targets, and prioritizing recommendations accordingly, as in rule-based advice that targets each user's lowest-scoring diet-quality components [7].

## 3. Target Users

**Primary users.** Adults aged 18–60 years with overweight or class I obesity (BMI 25.0–34.9 kg/m²) who own an Android or iOS smartphone, can walk for 10 minutes, and do not require clinical supervision for weight management.

**Excluded users.** People with diagnosed diabetes or cardiovascular disease, pregnant women, people currently taking weight-loss medication, and people with contraindications on the PAR-Q+ pre-exercise screening questionnaire. Because the system runs without clinician supervision and EASO recommends that nutrition therapy be delivered within multidisciplinary teams [2], the application refers such people to clinical care instead of giving recommendations.

**Secondary stakeholders.** The research team (pilot-study administrators who need pseudonymized, exportable engagement and outcome data) and the supervisor.

**Pilot setting.** Participants will be recruited from the Astana IT University community and the wider Astana population after ethics approval (see Section 8).

---

## 4. Aim and Research Questions

**Aim.** To design, implement and evaluate a transparent, rule-based mobile recommender system that identifies each user's modifiable obesity-related lifestyle factors and jointly personalizes nutrition and physical activity recommendations for adults with overweight or class I obesity.

**Primary research question.** To what extent does a stand-alone mobile recommender system that jointly personalizes nutrition and physical activity recommendations through transparent, guideline-constrained rules improve 12-week engagement and weight-related outcomes in adults with overweight or class I obesity, compared with a self-monitoring-only version of the same application?

**Sub-question (SQ1).** Do the recommendations generated by the rule engine satisfy predefined nutritional and physical activity constraints across representative user profiles?

## 5. Objectives

Each objective has a deliverable and a measurable success criterion. The criteria are the ones used in the verification and evaluation plan (pre-defense item 9).

| ID | Objective | Deliverable | Success criterion / measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **O1** | Formalize an explainable rule base that identifies each user's deviating lifestyle factors and converts dietary guidance [2], [7] and exercise evidence into personalized energy, macronutrient, nutrient-quality and activity targets with intensity limits. | Documented rule base: for each rule, its inputs, output, constraint and evidence source. | Every rule is traceable to a cited guideline or study; every recommendation shown to the user displays the rule that produced it. |
| **O2** | Develop a cross-platform prototype that integrates the rule engine, food logging, wearable data and personalized-threshold prompts, and verify its outputs against the constraints (SQ1). | Mobile client (Android and iOS), backend API and database, rule engine, verification test suite. | 100% of plans generated for 200 synthetic profiles (spanning age, sex, BMI and activity level) satisfy the hard constraints: energy within the target range, macronutrient shares within guideline ranges, calcium adequacy, intensity not above the profile's current zone. Failures trigger rule correction and re-testing. |
| **O3** | Compare engagement between the recommender-system arm and the self-monitoring control arm over 12 weeks. | Engagement dataset from time-stamped app logs; statistical analysis. | Proportion of active days over 84 days (H1); retention (completion of the week-12 visit); prompt response (recorded activity within 60 minutes of a prompt); System Usability Scale (SUS) score at week 12. |
| **O4** | Compare weight-related outcomes and safety between the arms. | Outcome dataset from weeks 0, 6 and 12; statistical analysis. | Percentage weight change at week 12 (H2); change in mean daily steps from baseline to weeks 11–12 (H3); waist circumference; adverse events reported in weekly check-ins. |

**Hypotheses** (two-sided tests, α = 0.05):

- **H1 (O3):** The recommender-system arm will have a higher proportion of active days over the 84-day period than the control arm.
- **H2 (O4):** The recommender-system arm will achieve a greater mean percentage reduction in body weight at week 12.
- **H3 (O4):** The recommender-system arm will show a greater increase in mean daily steps from baseline to weeks 11–12.

SQ1 is a verification question and has no hypothesis; its success criterion is given under O2.

## 6. Expected Outcomes

1. **A verifiable rule base** that identifies modifiable lifestyle factors and jointly personalizes nutrition and physical activity targets, with each rule linked to its evidence source.
2. **A working cross-platform prototype**: mobile client, backend and rule engine, with optional wearable integration, released in the project repository with setup and test instructions.
3. **A verification report** showing the result of constraint checks on 200 synthetic profiles (SQ1).
4. **Pilot effect estimates** for engagement, weight-related outcomes and safety from a 12-week, two-arm randomized pilot with a planned 60 participants (30 per arm). With 30 per arm the study can detect only effects of about d = 0.74 at 80% power; it is therefore a pilot whose estimates will size a later confirmatory study, not a confirmatory trial.
5. **The diploma thesis and repository documentation** (requirements, architecture, evaluation results) describing the above.

## 7. Project Scope

### 7.1 In scope

- Onboarding questionnaire and eligibility screening (age, BMI, PAR-Q+), with referral to clinical care for ineligible users.
- User profile: age, sex, height, weight, waist circumference, activity level.
- Identification of modifiable lifestyle factors (Section 2.3) from the profile and logged data.
- Food logging using a food database that includes locally consumed foods; weight logging.
- Step data from the phone and, optionally, from wearables through Google Health Connect and Apple HealthKit.
- Rule-based recommendations: weekly update of energy and macronutrient targets from logged intake and weight trend; meal suggestions; flags for low diet-quality components and low calcium intake; step targets raised gradually from the user's baseline week; low-intensity heart-rate zones when a wearable is connected; two resistance sessions per week; prompts to interrupt prolonged sitting.
- An explanation for every recommendation (the rule that produced it).
- Personalized-threshold activity prompts (push notifications).
- A self-monitoring-only mode of the same application for the control arm (tracking only, no recommendations or prompts).
- Weekly in-app adverse-event check-in.
- Engagement logging and pseudonymized data export for analysis.
- Verification of the rule engine on synthetic profiles and a 12-week randomized pilot study.

### 7.2 Out of scope

- Medical diagnosis of obesity, its causes or comorbidities, and any clinical decision-making.
- Medication, supplements or any change to medical treatment.
- Users who require medical management (see exclusion criteria in Section 3), people with BMI ≥ 35.0 kg/m², children and adolescents.
- Assessment of genetic, microbiome, clinical laboratory or socioeconomic factors.
- Recommendations generated by opaque models (for example, large language models) that cannot be traced to a rule.
- Real-time human coaching, telemedicine or integration with clinical information systems.
- Commercial release in app stores, payments and marketing.
- A confirmatory efficacy trial or long-term (beyond 12 weeks) weight-maintenance evaluation.

## 8. Engineering Complexity

The project combines several technically distinct components that must work together and be verified:

| Component | Engineering challenge |
|---|---|
| **Mobile client** (cross-platform, Android and iOS; Flutter planned) | Onboarding and screening flow, food and weight logging, recommendation and explanation screens, local notifications, offline-tolerant logging, two application modes (intervention and control) from one code base. |
| **Backend and API** | Authentication, user profiles, storage of time-series data (intake, weight, steps), scheduled weekly target updates, randomization and arm assignment, pseudonymized export. |
| **Rule engine** | Formal representation of guideline-based rules; deterministic, explainable outputs; hard-constraint checking; versioning of rules so that every recommendation can be traced to a rule version. |
| **Factor identification** | Combining profile and logged data to rank deviating lifestyle factors per user and to prioritize recommendations. |
| **Wearable integration** | Reading steps and heart rate through Google Health Connect and Apple HealthKit, handling permissions, missing data and differences between platforms. |
| **Personalized prompts** | Deriving prompt thresholds from each user's baseline week and scheduling context-aware notifications. |
| **Data protection** | Health data are sensitive: pseudonymization, data minimization, encryption in transit and at rest, no personal data in the repository. |
| **Verification and evaluation** | Automated generation of 200 synthetic profiles and constraint checks (SQ1); instrumentation of engagement metrics; statistical analysis of the pilot (intention-to-treat, Python statsmodels). |

The final technology stack is selected and justified in the technology comparison (pre-defense item 5) and recorded in `docs/architecture/`.
